## 9.5 Unitary representations of the braid group

We have already noted that the angular momentum spectrum has different properties in two spatial dimensions than in three dimensions because $\text{SO}(2)$ has different topological properties than $\text{SO}(3)$ ($\text{SO}(3)$ has a compact simply connected covering group $\text{SU}(2)$, but $\text{SO}(2)$ does not). This observation provides one way to see why anyons are possible in two dimensions but not in three. It is also instructive to observe that particle exchanges have different topological properties in two spatial dimensions than in three dimensions.

As we have found in our discussion of the relation between the statistics of particles and of antiparticles, it is useful to envision exchanges of particles as processes taking place in spacetime. In particular, it is convenient to imagine that we are computing the quantum transition amplitude for a time-dependent process involving $n$ particles by evaluating a sum over particle histories (though for our purposes it will not actually be necessary to calculate any path integrals).

Consider a system of $n$ indistinguishable pointlike particles confined to a two-dimensional spatial surface (which for now we may assume is the plane), and suppose that no two particles are permitted to occupy coincident positions. We may think of a configuration of the particles at a fixed time as a plane with $n$ "punctures" at specified locations --- that is, we associate with each particle a hole in the surface with infinitesimal radius. The condition that the particles are forbidden to coincide is enforced by demanding that there are exactly $n$ punctures in the plane at any time. Furthermore, just as the particles are indistinguishable, each puncture is the same as any other. Thus if we were to perform a permutation of the $n$ punctures, this would have no physical effect; all the punctures are the same anyway, so it makes no difference which one is which. All that matters is the $n$ distinct particle positions in the plane.

<!-- Page 14 -->
To evaluate the quantum amplitude for a configuration of $n$ particles at specified initial positions at time $t = 0$ to evolve to a configuration of $n$ particles at specified final positions at time $t = T$, we are to sum over all classical histories for the $n$ particles that interpolate between the fixed initial configuration and the fixed final configuration, weighted by the phase $e^{iS}$, where $S$ is the classical action of the history. If we envision each particle world line as a thread, each history for the $n$ particles becomes a braid, where each particle on the initial ($t = 0$) time slice can be connected by a thread to any one of the particles on the final ($t = T$) time slice. Furthermore, since the particle world lines are forbidden to cross, the braids fall into distinct topological classes that cannot be smoothly deformed one to another, and the path integral can be decomposed as a sum of contributions, with each contribution arising from a different topological class of histories.

Nontrivial exchange operations acting on the particles on the final time slice change the topological class of the braid. Thus we see that the elements of the symmetry group generated by exchanges are in one-to-one correspondence with the topological classes. This (infinite) group is called the *$n$-string braid group*, and is denoted $B_n$.

A presentation of $B_n$ can be formulated in terms of the operations that exchange adjacent particles. Let $\sigma_j$ denote a counterclockwise exchange of the particle currently in the $j$th position with the particle currently in the $(j+1)$st position, where $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, n-1\}$. Then $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_{n-1}$ are the group generators. The defining relations satisfied by these generators are of two types. The first type is

$$
\sigma_j \sigma_k = \sigma_k \sigma_j, \quad |j - k| \ge 2,
$$ {#eq-8.13}

which just says that exchanges of disjoint pairs of particles commute. The second, slightly more subtle, type of relation is

$$
\sigma_j \sigma_{j+1} \sigma_j = \sigma_{j+1} \sigma_j \sigma_{j+1}, \quad j = 1, 2, \dots, n-2,
$$ {#eq-8.14}

which is sometimes called the *Yang-Baxter relation*.

<!-- Page 15 -->
The Yang-Baxter relation can be verified by visualizing how threads can be moved in space. The operation $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sigma_1$ on three particles (numbered 1, 2, 3 from left to right) means that first 1 and 2 are exchanged, then 2 and 3 (the particles now in the second and third positions), and then 1 and 2 again. The operation $\sigma_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2$ means that first 2 and 3 are exchanged, then 1 and 2, and then 2 and 3 again. Laying out the braids corresponding to both sequences of exchanges, one sees that in both cases the thread connecting initial position 1 to final position 3 passes under the other two threads, the thread connecting 3 to 1 passes over the other two threads, and the thread connecting 2 to 2 passes over one thread and under the other. Furthermore, the braid for $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sigma_1$ can be smoothly deformed to the braid for $\sigma_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2$ by sliding the thread connecting 1 and 3 across the thread connecting 2 and 2, keeping the endpoints fixed and without any threads crossing --- i.e., these are topologically equivalent braids.

– Figure: Braid diagrams illustrating the Yang-Baxter relation $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sigma_1 = \sigma_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2$ –

Since the braid group is infinite, it has an infinite number of unitary irreducible representations, and in fact there are an infinite number of one-dimensional representations. Indistinguishable particles that transform as a one-dimensional representation of the braid group are said to be *abelian anyons*. In the one-dimensional representations, each generator $\sigma_j$ of $B_n$ is represented by a phase $\sigma_j = e^{i\theta_j}$. Furthermore, the Yang-Baxter relation becomes $e^{i\theta_j} e^{i\theta_{j+1}} e^{i\theta_j} = e^{i\theta_{j+1}} e^{i\theta_j} e^{i\theta_{j+1}}$, which implies $e^{i\theta_j} = e^{i\theta_{j+1}} \equiv e^{i\theta}$ --- all exchanges are represented by the *same* phase. Of course, that makes sense; if the particles are really indistinguishable, the exchange phase ought not to depend on which pair is exchanged. For $\theta = 0$ we obtain bosons, and for $\theta = \pi$, fermions.

The braid group also has many nonabelian representations that are of dimension greater than one; indistinguishable particles that transform as such representations are said to be *nonabelian anyons* (or, sometimes, *nonabelions*). To understand the physical properties of nonabelian anyons we will need to understand the mathematical structure of some of these representations. In these lectures, I hope to convey some intuition about nonabelian anyons by discussing some examples in detail.

For now, though, we can already anticipate the main goal we hope to fulfill. For nonabelian anyons, the irreducible representation of $B_n$ realized by $n$ anyons acts on a "topological vector space" $V_n$ whose dimension $D_n$ increases exponentially with $n$. And for anyons with suitable properties, the image of the representation may be *dense* in $\text{SU}(D_n)$. Then braiding of anyons can simulate a quantum computation --- any (special) unitary transformation acting on the exponentially large vector space $V_n$ can be realized with arbitrarily good fidelity by executing a suitably chosen braid.

Thus we are keenly interested in the nonabelian representations of the braid group. But we should also emphasize (and will discuss at greater

<!-- Page 16 -->
length later on) that there is more to a model of anyons than a mere representation of the braid group. In our flux tube model of abelian anyons, we were able to describe not only the effects of an exchange of anyons, but also the types of particles that can be obtained when two or more anyons are combined together. Likewise, in a general anyon model, the anyons are of various types, and the model incorporates "fusion rules" that specify what types can be obtained when two anyons of particular types are combined. Nontrivial consistency conditions arise because fusion is associative (fusing $a$ with $b$ and then fusing the result with $c$ is equivalent to fusing $b$ with $c$ and then fusing the result with $a$), and because the fusion rules must be consistent with the braiding rules. Though these consistency conditions are highly restrictive, many solutions exist, and hence many different models of nonabelian anyons are realizable in principle.

## 9.6 Topological degeneracy

But before moving on to nonabelian anyons, there is another important idea concerning abelian anyons that we should discuss. In any model of anyons (indeed, in any local quantum system with a mass gap), there is a ground state or vacuum state, the state in which no particles are present. On the plane the ground state is unique, but for a two-dimensional surface with nontrivial topology, the ground state can be degenerate.

Consider an anyon model defined on a surface with the topology of a torus (genus $g = 1$). A torus has two independent noncontractible cycles, which we may denote $C_1$ and $C_2$. Let $T_1$ denote a unitary operator that creates an anyon-antianyon pair from the vacuum, transports the anyon around $C_1$, and then reannihilates the pair. Similarly, let $T_2$ denote a unitary operator that creates a pair, transports the anyon around $C_2$, and reannihilates the pair. If the Hamiltonian of the system is local, and the linear dimensions of the torus are large compared to the correlation length of the medium, then the operators $T_1$ and $T_2$ commute with the Hamiltonian. In fact, $T_1$ and $T_2$ change the state of the system only by modifying the holonomy around $C_1$ and $C_2$, which cannot be detected by any local measurement.

However, $T_1$ and $T_2$ do not commute with each other! Assuming the anyons are abelian (and that all

<!-- Page 17 -->
interactions among distantly separated anyons are due to the Aharonov-Bohm effect), then the commutator of $T_1$ and $T_2$ is

$$
T_2^{-1} T_1^{-1} T_2 T_1 = e^{-i 2	heta} I,
$$ {#eq-8.15}

where $e^{i	heta}$ is the anyon's exchange phase. The nontrivial commutator arises because the process in which (1) an anyon winds around $C_1$, (2) an anyon winds around $C_2$, (3) an anyon winds around $C_1$ in the reverse direction, and (4) an anyon winds around $C_2$ in the reverse direction, is topologically equivalent to a process in which one anyon winds clockwise around another. To verify this claim, view the action of $T_2^{-1} T_1^{-1} T_2 T_1$ as a process in spacetime. First note that the process described by the operator $T_1^{-1} T_1$, in which an anyon world line first sweeps through $C_1$ and then immediately traverses $C_1$ in the reverse order, can be deformed to a process in which the anyon world line traverses a topologically trivial loop that can be smoothly shrunk to a point (in keeping with the property that $T_1^{-1} T_1$ is really the identity operator). In similar fashion, the process described by the operator $T_2^{-1} T_1^{-1} T_2 T_1$ can be deformed to one where the anyon world lines traverse two closed loops, but such that the world lines *link* once with one another; furthermore, one loop pierces the surface bounded by the other loop in a direction opposite to the orientation inherited by the surface via the right-hand rule from its bounding loop. This process can be smoothly deformed to one in which two pairs are created, one anyon winds clockwise around the other, and then both pairs annihilate. The clockwise winding is equivalent to two successive clockwise exchanges, represented in our one-dimensional representation of the braid group by the phase $e^{-i 2	heta}$. We conclude that $T_1$ and $T_2$ are noncommuting, except in the cases $	heta = 0$ (bosons) and $	heta = \pi$ (fermions).

– Figure: Spacetime diagram showing the linking of two closed loops corresponding to $T_2^{-1} T_1^{-1} T_2 T_1$ –

<!-- Page 18 -->
Because $T_1$ and $T_2$ do not commute, they cannot be simultaneously diagonalized. Since $T_1$ is unitary, its eigenvalues are phases; let us use the angular variable $\alpha \in [0, 2\pi)$ to label an eigenstate of $T_1$ with eigenvalue $e^{i\alpha}$:

$$
T_1 |\alpha\rangle = e^{i\alpha} |\alpha\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.16}

Then applying $T_2$ to the $T_1$ eigenstate advances the value of $\alpha$ by $2\theta$:

$$
T_1 (T_2 |\alpha\rangle) = e^{i 2\theta} T_2 T_1 |\alpha\rangle = e^{i 2\theta} e^{i\alpha} (T_2 |\alpha\rangle).
$$ {#eq-8.17}

Suppose that $\theta$ is a rational multiple of $2\pi$, which we may express as

$$
\theta = \pi p / q,
$$ {#eq-8.18}

where $q$ and $p$ ($p < 2q$) are positive integers with no common factors. Then $T_2$ applied repeatedly must have at least $q$ distinct eigenvalues; $T_1$ acting on $\alpha$ generates an orbit with $q$ distinct values:

$$
\alpha + \frac{2\pi p}{q} k \pmod{2\pi}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, q-1.
$$ {#eq-8.19}

Since $T_1$ commutes with the Hamiltonian, each of these $q$ eigenstates of $T_1$ has the same energy. If $|\alpha\rangle$ is a ground state of the system, then so is $T_2^k |\alpha\rangle$ for each $k = 0, 1, \dots, q-1$. We conclude that the ground state on the torus is at least $q$-fold degenerate!

More generally, if the surface has genus $g$ (it is a sphere with $g$ handles), then there are $2g$ independent noncontractible cycles, and the ground state is at least $q^g$-fold degenerate. This degeneracy is *topological* --- it depends on the topology of the surface, but not on the geometry (the size or shape).

<!-- Page 19 -->
Topological degeneracy is a key property of anyon models that has profound implications for quantum information processing. Because the different ground states on the torus cannot be distinguished by any local measurement, they can serve as protected memory states for storing quantum information. Suppose we store a qubit in a two-dimensional subspace of the degenerate ground state space. How might the stored information be damaged? An error can occur only if a virtual anyon-antianyon pair fluctuates into existence, one member of the pair propagates all the way around a noncontractible cycle of the torus, and the pair reannihilates. If the linear size $L$ of the torus is large compared to the correlation length $\xi$ of the medium, the amplitude for such a process is suppressed by a factor of order $e^{-L/\xi}$. Therefore, the rate of errors in the stored quantum information is exponentially small in the system size!

Furthermore, the topological degeneracy is robust against small perturbations of the Hamiltonian. If we perturb the Hamiltonian by adding a local term $\delta H$, the energy splitting of the degenerate ground states is also suppressed by $e^{-L/\xi}$, because the perturbation must act at order $L/\xi$ in perturbation theory to induce an operator that connects different ground states. Thus, the energy degeneracy is "topologically protected."

## 9.7 Toric code revisited

<!-- Page 20 -->
We have already encountered this phenomenon of topological degeneracy in our study of the toric code in Chapter 7. Let us recall how it works in that context. The toric code can be viewed as the (degenerate) ground state of a system of qubits that occupy the links of a square lattice on the torus, with Hamiltonian

$$
H = -\frac{1}{4\Delta} \left( \sum_P Z_P + \sum_S X_S \right),
$$ {#eq-8.20}

where the plaquette operator $Z_P = \prod_{j \in P} Z_j$ is the product of Pauli $Z$ operators on the four links bounding the plaquette $P$, and the star operator $X_S = \prod_{j \in S} X_j$ is the product of Pauli $X$ operators on the four links incident on the site $S$. The ground state space is the space of states satisfying $Z_P = 1$ for all $P$ and $X_S = 1$ for all $S$.

Excitations above the ground state are anyons. A plaquette $P$ with $Z_P = -1$ is a "magnetic fluxon," and a site $S$ with $X_S = -1$ is an "electric chargeon." Both types of particles are bosons under self-exchange (their exchange phase is $\theta = 0$). However, they have nontrivial *mutual statistics*: if an electric chargeon is transported around a magnetic fluxon, the wave function acquires a phase $(-1)$.

<!-- Page 21 -->
On the torus, there are two independent noncontractible cycles $C_1$ and $C_2$. Let $T_{1,S}$ and $T_{2,S}$ denote the unitary operators that transport a chargeon around $C_1$ and $C_2$, and let $T_{1,P}$ and $T_{2,P}$ denote the operators that transport a fluxon around $C_1$ and $C_2$. The nontrivial commutators are

$$
T_{2,P}^{-1} T_{1,S}^{-1} T_{2,P} T_{1,S} = -1 = T_{2,S}^{-1} T_{1,P}^{-1} T_{2,S} T_{1,P},
$$ {#eq-8.21}

both arising from processes in which the world lines of a chargeon and a fluxon link once in spacetime. The other pairs of operators commute: $[T_{1,S}, T_{2,S}] = 0$ and $[T_{1,P}, T_{2,P}] = 0$.

From the algebra of these operators, we see that the ground state space on the torus must be 4-fold degenerate ($2^2 = 4$). We can choose a basis in which $T_{1,S}$ and $T_{1,P}$ are diagonal with eigenvalues $\pm 1$; then $T_{2,P}$ flips the eigenvalue of $T_{1,S}$, and $T_{2,S}$ flips the eigenvalue of $T_{1,P}$.

This 4-fold degeneracy encodes two protected logical qubits. Under local perturbations, such as a uniform magnetic field

$$
H' = -h \sum_\ell Z_\ell,
$$ {#eq-8.22}

the splitting between the four ground states is suppressed as $O(h^L)$, where $L$ is the linear size of the torus in lattice units.

## 9.8 The nonabelian Aharonov-Bohm effect

So far, we have discussed abelian anyons. Now we take the step to *nonabelian* anyons. To motivate the concept of nonabelian anyons, it is helpful to generalize the Aharonov-Bohm effect to nonabelian gauge groups.

<!-- Page 22 -->
In quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the gauge group is $\text{SU}(3)$, which is nonabelian. Quarks carry a "color" charge that transforms as the 3-dimensional fundamental representation of $\text{SU}(3)$. The color states can be denoted $|R\rangle, |Y\rangle, |B\rangle$ (red, yellow, blue). A general color state of a quark is a superposition

$$
|\psi_q\rangle = q_R |R\rangle + q_Y |Y\rangle + q_B |B\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.23}

Suppose a quark is transported adiabatically around a closed path $C$ enclosing a localized nonabelian magnetic field. Under parallel transport, the quark's color state is rotated by a unitary matrix $U$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} q'_R \\ q'_Y \\ q'_B \end{pmatrix} = U \begin{pmatrix} q_R \\ q_Y \\ q_B \end{pmatrix},
$$ {#eq-8.24}

where $U = \mathcal{P} \exp\left( i \oint_C A_\mu dx^\mu \right)$ is the Wilson loop (path-ordered exponential of the gauge field).

<!-- Page 23 -->
The matrix $U$ depends on the choice of basis at the reference basepoint $x_0$. Under a gauge transformation $V(x)$, the matrix $U(C, x_0)$ transforms by conjugation:

$$
U(C, x_0) \mapsto V(x_0) U(C, x_0) V(x_0)^{-1}.
$$ {#eq-8.25}

Therefore, the gauge-invariant property of the magnetic flux enclosed by the loop $C$ is not the matrix $U$ itself, but rather the *conjugacy class* to which $U$ belongs.

Now, imagine that the continuous gauge symmetry is broken down to a finite discrete group $G$ by the condensation of a Higgs field (a "nonabelian superconductor"). In such a system, there are no massless gauge bosons (all gauge fields acquire mass via the Higgs mechanism). The magnetic flux is quantized, and localized flux tubes ("fluxons") are stable particle-like excitations. Each fluxon is labeled by an element $a \in G$, but because of gauge ambiguity, only the conjugacy class $\alpha$ of $a$ is gauge-invariant.

At a reference basepoint $x_0$, let $\{|R, i\rangle\}_{i=1}^{|R|}$ denote an orthonormal basis for an irreducible representation $R$ of $G$:

$$
|R, i\rangle, \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, |R|.
$$ {#eq-8.26}

<!-- Page 24 -->
When a charged particle in representation $R$ is transported around a fluxon labeled by group element $a \in G$, its internal state is rotated by the representation matrix $D^R(a)$:

$$
|R, j\rangle \mapsto \sum_{i=1}^{|R|} |R, i\rangle D^R_{ij}(a).
$$ {#eq-8.27}

This is the *nonabelian Aharonov-Bohm effect*.

## 9.9 Braiding of nonabelian fluxons

Now let us consider the effect of *exchanging* two nonabelian fluxons. Suppose we have two fluxons, one at position 1 and the other at position 2, and we have calibrated them using standard paths $\alpha$ and $\beta$ originating from a basepoint $x_0$, winding counterclockwise around fluxon 1 and fluxon 2 respectively. Under this calibration, fluxon 1 has flux $a \in G$ and fluxon 2 has flux $b \in G$, so the state can be denoted $|a, b\rangle$.

<!-- Page 25 -->
Now imagine that we perform a counterclockwise exchange of the two fluxons, while continuously deforming the reference paths so that they never cross the fluxons. As shown in the figure, under this exchange the path $\alpha\beta\alpha^{-1}$ is deformed into $\alpha$, and the path $\alpha$ is deformed into $\beta$:

$$
\alpha\beta\alpha^{-1} \mapsto \alpha, \quad \alpha \mapsto \beta.
$$ {#eq-8.28}

– Figure: Deformation of reference paths under a counterclockwise exchange of two fluxons –

<!-- Page 26 -->
Therefore, after the exchange, the fluxon that is now at position 2 has flux $a$, and the fluxon that is now at position 1 has flux $a b a^{-1}$. Thus, the braid operator $R$ representing a counterclockwise exchange acts on the two-fluxon state according to

$$
R : |a, b\rangle \mapsto |aba^{-1}, a\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.29}

The inverse operator $R^{-1}$, representing a clockwise exchange, acts as

$$
R^{-1} : |a, b\rangle \mapsto |b, b^{-1}ab\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.30}

The *monodromy* operator $R^2$, representing a complete counterclockwise winding of one fluxon around the other, acts as

$$
R^2 : |a, b\rangle \mapsto |(ab)a(ab)^{-1}, (ab)b(ab)^{-1}\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.31}

Both fluxes are conjugated by the total flux $c = ab$. Note that if the group $G$ is abelian, $aba^{-1} = b$, so $R : |a, b\rangle \mapsto |b, a\rangle$, which is just a permutation. But for a nonabelian group, the fluxes are actively transformed by conjugation!

As an example, consider the permutation group $G = S_3$, the smallest nonabelian group. $S_3$ has 6 elements: the identity $e$, three two-cycles $(12), (23), (31)$, and two three-cycles $(123), (132)$.

Consider two fluxons whose fluxes belong to the conjugacy class of two-cycles. In this subspace, $R$ has orbits of length three:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R : |(12), (23)\rangle &\mapsto |(31), (12)\rangle \mapsto |(23), (31)\rangle \mapsto |(12), (23)\rangle, \\
R : |(23), (12)\rangle &\mapsto |(31), (23)\rangle \mapsto |(12), (31)\rangle \mapsto |(23), (12)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-8.32}

<!-- Page 27 -->
Diagonalizing $R$ on the orbit of $|(12), (23)\rangle$, we find the eigenstates and eigenvalues:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R = 1 &: |(12), (23)\rangle + |(31), (12)\rangle + |(23), (31)\rangle, \\
R = \omega &: |(12), (23)\rangle + \bar{\omega}|(31), (12)\rangle + \omega|(23), (31)\rangle, \\
R = \bar{\omega} &: |(12), (23)\rangle + \omega|(31), (12)\rangle + \bar{\omega}|(23), (31)\rangle,
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-8.33}

where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ and $\bar{\omega} = e^{-2\pi i/3}$.

Next, consider a pair of fluxons with trivial total flux $ab = e$, so $b = a^{-1}$. A state $|a, a^{-1}\rangle$ has zero total flux. If we transport a third fluxon with flux $b$ around this pair, the state transforms as

$$
|a, a^{-1}\rangle \mapsto |bab^{-1}, ba^{-1}b^{-1}\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.34}

For each conjugacy class $\alpha$, there is a unique state $|0; \alpha\rangle$ that is invariant under conjugation by all $b \in G$, representing a fluxon-antifluxon pair with *zero total charge*:

$$
|0; \alpha\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\alpha|}} \sum_{a \in \alpha} |a, a^{-1}\rangle,
$$ {#eq-8.35}

where $|\alpha|$ denotes the order of the conjugacy class.

<!-- Page 28 -->
In the case $G = S_3$ and $\alpha$ the two-cycle class, the subspace of states with trivial total flux has dimension 3, spanned by $|(12), (12)\rangle, |(23), (23)\rangle, |(31), (31)\rangle$. The state $|0; \alpha\rangle$ is the symmetric combination. The orthogonal 2-dimensional subspace has nontrivial charge, spanned by

$$
\begin{aligned}
|0\rangle &= |(12), (12)\rangle + \bar{\omega}|(23), (23)\rangle + \omega|(31), (31)\rangle, \\
|1\rangle &= |(12), (12)\rangle + \omega|(23), (23)\rangle + \bar{\omega}|(31), (31)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-8.36}

Under conjugation by $S_3$, this two-dimensional subspace transforms as the 2-dimensional irreducible representation $[2]$ of $S_3$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D(12) &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
D(23) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \bar{\omega} \\ \omega & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
D(31) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \omega \\ \bar{\omega} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\
D(123) &= \begin{pmatrix} \omega & 0 \\ 0 & \bar{\omega} \end{pmatrix}, \quad
D(132) = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{\omega} & 0 \\ 0 & \omega \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-8.37}

Similarly, a pair of charged particles ("chargeons") in representations $R$ and $\bar{R}$ can be in a state of trivial total charge:

$$
|0; R\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|R|}} \sum_i |R, i\rangle \otimes |\bar{R}, i\rangle.
$$ {#eq-8.38}

If one member of a fluxon pair with flux $a \in \alpha$ winds around one member of the chargeon pair, the state of the chargeon pair becomes

$$
|0; R\rangle \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{|R|}} \sum_{i,j} |R, j\rangle \otimes |\bar{R}, i\rangle D^R_{ji}(a).
$$ {#eq-8.39}

The probability that the chargeon pair is measured to still have zero total charge is the square of the overlap:

$$
\text{Prob}(0) = \left| \frac{\chi^R(a)}{|R|} \right|^2,
$$ {#eq-8.40}

where

<!-- Page 29 -->
$$
\chi^R(a) = \sum_i D^R_{ii}(a) = \operatorname{tr} D^R(a)
$$ {#eq-8.41}

is the character of the representation $R$ evaluated at $a$. When $\chi^R(a) < |R|$, charge is transferred between the fluxon pair and the chargeon pair: the linking of their world lines induces entanglement between the two pairs!

## 9.10 Superselection sectors of a nonabelian superconductor

In a nonabelian superconductor with discrete gauge group $G$, what are all the possible particle types (superselection sectors)?

A particle excitation can carry both magnetic flux and electric charge. The magnetic flux is specified by a conjugacy class $\alpha$ of $G$. But for a particle with a fixed flux $a \in \alpha$, what is its charge? The charge cannot be an arbitrary representation of $G$, because a gauge transformation that does not commute with $a$ would change the flux of the particle. The gauge transformations that leave the flux $a$ invariant form a subgroup of $G$ called the *normalizer* (or *centralizer*) of $a$ in $G$:

$$
N(a) = \{b \in G \mid ab = ba\}.
$$ {#eq-8.42}

Therefore, the charge of a particle with flux $a$ is classified by an irreducible representation $R(a)$ of $N(a)$. The superselection sector is thus labeled by the pair $(\alpha, R(\alpha))$, where $\alpha$ is a conjugacy class of $G$ and $R(\alpha)$ is an irreducible representation of the normalizer group $N(\alpha)$.

<!-- Page 30 -->
The *quantum dimension* $d_{(\alpha, R(\alpha))}$ of the particle is the dimension of the Hilbert space of a single localized particle, which is

$$
d_{(\alpha, R(\alpha))} = |\alpha| \cdot |R(\alpha)|.
$$

The total quantum dimension $\mathcal{D}$ is defined by summing the squares of the quantum dimensions over all particle types:

$$
\mathcal{D}^2 = \sum_\alpha \sum_{R(\alpha)} d_{(\alpha, R(\alpha))}^2 = \sum_\alpha |\alpha|^2 \sum_{R(\alpha)} |R(\alpha)|^2.
$$ {#eq-8.43}

Since the sum of the squared dimensions of all irreducible representations of a finite group equals the order of the group, and the order of the normalizer is $|N(\alpha)| = |G|/|\alpha|$, we obtain

$$
\mathcal{D}^2 = \sum_\alpha |\alpha|^2 \cdot \frac{|G|}{|\alpha|} = \sum_\alpha |\alpha| \cdot |G| = |G|^2,
$$ {#eq-8.44}

so the total dimension is $\mathcal{D} = |G|$.

For the case $G = S_3$, there are 8 particle types:

| Type | Flux | Charge | Dim |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| A | $e$ | $[+]$ | 1 |
| B | $e$ | $[-]$ | 1 |
| C | $e$ | $[2]$ | 2 |
| D | $(12)$ | $[+]$ | 3 |
| E | $(12)$ | $[-]$ | 3 |
| F | $(123)$ | $[1]$ | 2 |
| G | $(123)$ | $[\omega]$ | 2 |
| H | $(123)$ | $[\bar{\omega}]$ | 2 |

If the flux is trivial ($e$), the normalizer is $S_3$, and the charge can be any of the three irreducible representations of $S_3$: trivial $[+]$ (dim 1), sign $[-]$ (dim 1), or standard $[2]$ (dim 2). If the flux is a two-cycle $(12)$, the normalizer is $Z_2 = \{e, (12)\}$, and the charge is either the trivial representation $[+]$ or nontrivial representation $[-]$ of $Z_2$ (dim $3 \times 1 = 3$). If the flux is a three-cycle $(123)$, the normalizer is $Z_3 = \{e, (123), (132)\}$, and the charge is one of the three 1D representations $[1], [\omega], [\bar{\omega}]$ of $Z_3$ (dim $2 \times 1 = 2$). The total dimension is $\mathcal{D} = |S_3| = 6$.

Since $a \in N(a)$ commutes with all elements of $N(a)$, by Schur's lemma the representation matrix $D^{R(a)}(a)$ is a multiple of the identity:

$$
D^{R(a)}(a) = \exp(i\theta_{R(a)}) I.
$$ {#eq-8.45}

A rotation of the composite object counterclockwise by $2\pi$ transports the charge around the flux, generating the topological spin:

$$
e^{-2\pi i \boldsymbol{J}} = e^{i\theta_{R(a)}}.
$$ {#eq-8.46}

When two particle types are fused together, the composite object can belong to various types, determined by the fusion rules of the theory. For $G = S_3$, the fusion rule for two particles of type $D$ is

$$
D \times D = A + C + F + G + H.
$$ {#eq-8.47}

For a system of two anyons, the monodromy operator $R^2$ representing counterclockwise winding of one particle around another satisfies

$$
(R_{ab}^c)^2 = e^{-2\pi i J_c} e^{2\pi i J_a} e^{2\pi i J_b} = e^{i(\theta_c - \theta_a - \theta_b)}.
$$ {#eq-8.48}

Here $R_{ab}^c$ denotes the braid operator for a counterclockwise exchange of particles of types $a$ and $b$ that are combined together into a composite of type $c$. Since each superselection sector has a definite topological spin, the monodromy operator is diagonal in the topological spin basis, and the eigenstates of the braid operator coincide with charge eigenstates.
